## 2. The method: an exact relation between two captures

At a 44.1 kHz host the plug-in does not convert (packet `first_order`): the
host signal is the network input, and the network output leaves 44 samples
later. So a capture at any other rate can be predicted from a capture at
44.1 kHz of the *converted* stimulus:

1. `u = to_internal(x)`: the internal stream the input converter would make of
   `x`, on internal indices counted from the first processed sample;
2. render `u` at a 44.1 kHz host with the same settings: its output, moved by
   the 44 samples, is the network output `y` on the same internal indices
   (the oscillators count internal samples, so the network is in the same
   state in both captures);
3. `to_host(y)` must equal the capture at the host rate, sample for sample.

The relation tests both converters, their delays and their time origin at the
precision of the reference's single-precision arithmetic, on any stimulus and
over the whole response. Stimuli: the campaign's programme with seed 4242
(impulses, a noise burst, four plucked tones, 14 s) and a 1 s probe (an impulse
on each input and a 30 ms noise burst).

## 3. The law

`rate / 44100 = L / M` in lowest terms. Host and internal samples meet on a
lattice of `1/L` internal sample = `1/M` host sample: host sample `n` sits at
`M n`, internal sample `m` at `L m`. Indices count from the first sample the
plug-in processes. `f = 44100 / rate = M / L`.

### 3.1 Reported latency

MEASURED at 48 rates, no exception:

    latency = 4 * floor(11 * rate / 44100 + 0.5)        host samples

It is 44 internal samples expressed at the host rate and rounded to a multiple
of 4, not "1 ms": 44, 48, 88, 96, 176, 192, 352, 384 at the usual rates, but 48
at 50 kHz, 68 at 66.15 kHz, 112 at 110.25 kHz. The thresholds were bracketed to
1 Hz: 50113 -> 48 and 50114 -> 52 (rule: 50113.6), 58131 -> 56 and 58132 -> 60
(58131.8), 66149 -> 64 and 66150 -> 68. The three exact ties in the set (66150,
110250, 154350 Hz) round up.

### 3.2 The kernel

One table serves both converters, as packet `first_order` found at 48 kHz:

    table[i] = 0.9 sinc(0.9 i / 4096) * I0(6 sqrt(1 - (i / 69631)^2)) / I0(6),      i = 0 ... 69631

read **without interpolation**, at the entry at or below the wanted distance.

- Input converter (host to internal, a down-conversion at every measured rate).
  The table is stepped by `dh = 4096 f` entries per host sample, so a tap at a
  distance of `d` host samples reads the entry at `4096 f d`: the kernel spans
  17 *internal* samples per wing (17/f host samples: 18.5 at 48 kHz, 74 at
  192 kHz, 148 at 384 kHz) and its cut-off is 0.9 of the *internal* Nyquist
  frequency. The sum is scaled by `f`.
- Output converter (internal to host, an up-conversion): the table is stepped
  by 4096 entries per internal sample, gain 1.
- So in both converters the 17 crossings and the 0.9 refer to the internal
  rate, which is also the lower of the two rates at every rate that can be
  measured. MEASURED. Which of the two readings is the rule cannot be decided
  above 44.1 kHz; section 6 gives the INFERRED answer.
- MEASURED, one element changed at a time, exact relation on the probe
  (`ablations` in the data file; `=` means the change does nothing at that rate):

  why the table size hardly matters there.

  score the same). The entries of `converters.py` are single precision, as in
  packet `first_order`. INFERRED.
- The DC gain of the kernel is not 1: through both converters of the model a
  constant comes back 1.0006 to 1.0008 times as large (+0.005 to +0.007 dB),
  depending on the rate and the phase. It follows from the table and is part
  of the law.

### 3.3 Time base: the lattice delays

MEASURED (exact relation, and the two delay rows of the table above):

    internal sample m is centred at host time     t_host = (m + 1) / f - (X_in + 1)
    host output sample N reads internal time      t_int  = (N - D) f - 28

    X_in = floor(18 / f + 10)                       host samples
    D    = latency       when rate / 44100 is a whole number (M = 1)
           2 * latency   otherwise

In lattice steps: a host impulse at `n` is centred at `M n + a`, host output
sample `N` reads the network output at `M N - b`, with

    a = (X_in + 1) M - L          b = 28 L + D M

- 18 is the number of zero crossings plus one and 10 a fixed margin:
  `X_in = floor(18 max(1, 1/f) + 10)` is the look-ahead of a converter in its
  own input samples, and 28 is the same expression for the output converter,
  whose input is the internal stream. INFERRED as the origin of the numbers;
  the numbers themselves are MEASURED at 19 rates.
- At 44.1 kHz there are no converters: `a = 0`, `b = 44`.
- The network keeps its own 44 internal samples before the lines at every rate
  (packet `first_order`), so a line of length `P` internal samples arrives at
  raw host sample `(a + b + (44 + P) L) / M`. With `P = 0` this is the fixed
  part that packet `laws` measured.

Delays in lattice steps and in internal samples ("int."). The other eleven

latency 68, `a` = 73, `b` = 356, fixed part 280.5 samples = 4.2404 ms, clocks

- The sum `a + b` is sharp to one lattice step (-41 to -54 dB for one step).
  The split is sharp to one internal sample (-48 dB): it moves the read of the
  modulated lines against their oscillators.
- Packet `laws` read the fixed part as 88.06, 203.34, 277.12 and 396.69 samples
  with its phase-slope estimator; the exact values are 88, 203.2789, 277 and
  396.5578. Its "read lead" of 72.1 and 116.2 internal samples at 88.2 and
  96 kHz is `b` (72 and 116.2).
- INFERRED: the plug-in works in blocks of `latency` host samples, which costs
  one block of delay at every rate (the reported latency; the dry path is
  delayed by it). With a ratio that is not a whole number the blocks do not
  give the same number of samples every time, and a second block of delay
  absorbs that. Not tested beyond the rule itself, which is MEASURED at four
  whole ratios (2, 3, 4, 8) and fifteen others, 3/2, 5/2 and 5/4 among them.

### 3.4 Exact table entries: the converter clocks

A tap whose distance falls exactly on a table entry is the one place where the
truncating lookup depends on rounding. It concerns every tap at 88.2, 176.4
and 352.8 kHz, one phase in three at 66.15 and 132.3 kHz, one in five at 48,
64, 96, 192 and 384 kHz, one in 125 at 50 and 100 kHz. Without a rule for it the model
stops at -71 to -76 dB behind the network (last column of the table in
section 1). Three rules settle it, all MEASURED:

1. **The clock of a converter decides.** Each converter keeps the time of its
   next output sample, in samples of its input, and advances it by `1/factor`.
   - A clock that is a hair low takes the entry *below* the exact one on the
     wing of inputs before the output sample; the other wing is exact.
   - A clock that is a hair high does so on the wing of inputs after it.
   - A clock whose step is a binary fraction is exact and lowers nothing by
     itself: the input clock at 88.2, 132.3, 176.4, 352.8, 66.15, 110.25 and
     55.125 kHz (steps 2, 3, 4, 8, 3/2, 5/2, 5/4 host samples), the output clock
     at 88.2, 176.4 and 352.8 kHz (steps 1/2, 1/4, 1/8 internal sample).

   The signs at 19 rates (table above and `rates[...].clockSigns`): for every
   rate both other values of each sign were tried on the probe; the right one
   scores -127.0 to -129.1 dB, the 76 wrong ones -70.7 to -93.6 dB.
2. **In the down-converter the table position is accumulated**, in double
   precision: `Ho = phase * dh`, then `Ho += dh` per tap, entry `int(Ho)`. When
   the clock is exact but `dh` is not a binary fraction (66.15, 132.3, 55.125,
   110.25 kHz), the rounding of that sum lowers single taps: at 132.3 kHz
   (`dh = 4096 / 3`, phase 0) taps 6, 15, 18, 21, 24 and 27 of the 51 of a
   wing, out of the 17 that fall on an exact entry. With the
   mathematically exact position instead: -77.3 dB (66.15 kHz) and -82.0 dB
   (132.3 kHz) against -128 dB.
3. **The wing of inputs after the output sample never uses the last table
   entry** (entry 69631, which a lowered tap at exactly 17 periods would
   read). Because the table ends in a step, keeping it costs -77.6 dB at 66.15
   and 132.3 kHz and -93 to -99 dB at 48, 96 and 192 kHz.

What sets the sign of a clock. INFERRED, with a MEASURED outcome:
`converters.clock_signs` runs the bookkeeping of a block converter of the
classic kind in double precision: the time starts at the converter's
look-ahead, runs while it is below the look-ahead plus the samples received,
is then moved back by those samples, and whole samples of creep are carried
into the next block. The input converter is fed `latency` host samples per
block, the output converter the internal samples that block produced. Because
the time stays between the look-ahead and the look-ahead plus one block, each
addition rounds the step to the grid of that range, always the same way, so
the clock drifts steadily to one side; the sign is final after at most two
blocks and unchanged after 100 s of simulated time.

- The simulation gives the measured pair of signs at **all 16 rates that have
  a drifting clock**, and "exact" at the three that have none.
- It singles out the block: one size for all rates gives at most 14 of 16
  (blocks of 44 or 48; 131 sizes from 8 to 2048 tried); half the latency 13,
  twice the latency 6, `round(rate / 1000)` 15. `latency - 1` also gives 16, so
  the block is the reported latency give or take a sample. Starting the
  converters from a primed state instead of at rest changes no sign.
- The classic kind: packet `first_order` already noted that the constants are
  those of the public-domain Kaiser-window sampling-rate converter in its
  high-quality setting. The three rules above, the look-ahead `18 + 10` and the
  block bookkeeping were *proposed* from my memory of that design and then
  tested against captures; no source code or documentation was consulted, and
  everything stated as MEASURED rests on the nulls alone.

### 3.5 What else depends on the host rate

- In the wet path at Macro 0: **nothing** (section 2: -131 dB with nothing but
  the converters between the two captures). Line lengths, modulation, decay,
  the fixed filter and the feedback all live at 44.1 kHz; it is the same
  network at every host rate.
- Outside the wet path, from packet `io` (not re-measured here): the
  pre-delay is a whole number of *host* samples in front of the input
  converter, the dry path is delayed by the reported latency, the ducking time
  constants are in milliseconds.
- Consequence: at Mix below 100 % the wet arrives later than the dry by the
  fixed part minus the latency (column "wet later" above, for a line of length
  0): 1.0 ms at 44.1 kHz, 3.2 ms at 48 kHz, 2.1 ms at 88.2 kHz, 3.1 ms at 96
  and 192 kHz. Against 44.1 kHz the whole pattern is 1.05 to 1.15 ms later at
  the whole ratios and 2.05 to 2.28 ms later at the other rates.

## 4. `converters.py`

    first, internal = to_internal(host, host_rate, origin)
    host = to_host(internal, host_rate, first, start, stop)

Both work on the lattice with whole numbers: one branch of coefficients per
phase (M branches into the network, L out of it), built once per rate by the
rules of section 3 with the clock signs of `clock_signs`. There is no running
clock, so a signal can be converted from any time origin. `describe(rate)`
gives the constants of a rate. For a product this is also the simplest exact
form: a polyphase filter with a counter modulo M or L.

With the network of `first_order_model.py` between the two functions the 48 kHz
model of wave 1 is reproduced within -101 to -121 dB; the difference is rule 3
of section 3.4, which wave 1 did not have (its exact output samples stayed
0.6 dB behind the others; the 48 kHz train now scores -80.39 dB against -80.22
with the wave 1 lookup).

## 5. How the rules were found, and what failed on the way

The numbers of this section come from exploratory scripts in the scratch
folder (`s05` to `s16`, model `hostmodel.py`, the wave 1 lookup with a wing
rule) and are not regenerated by `fit_converters.py`.

- 88.2 and 176.4 kHz fell at once: the 48 kHz kernel, stretched for the input
  and with the gain `f`, scored -80.46 and -80.37 dB with the delays found by a
  search over +-3 and +-4 host samples and with *no* rule for exact entries
  (any wing rule: -62 to -71 dB).
- The delay formulas were read off 48, 88.2 and 176.4 kHz (input 4250/147, 45
  and 79 host samples = `X_in + 1` host samples minus one internal sample).
  They predicted the sums at 96 and 192 kHz to the lattice step, and 50, 64 and
  66.15 kHz with a lag of 0.000 samples in a cross-correlation over +-3 ms. At
  132.3 kHz the same guess of two blocks was off by exactly 132 samples, one
  block: the whole-ratio case that 88.2 and 176.4 kHz had shown.
- **The wing rule of 48 kHz is not general.** At 96 kHz it holds (-80.31 dB;
  none -74.70). At 192 kHz it scores -73.87 dB, worse than none (-75.06); the
  opposite rule scores -80.39 dB. At 64 kHz both wings are the "before" wing
  (-80.38 dB). This is what led to the clocks.
- **A false twin.** At 192 kHz the 48 kHz rule with both delays moved by two
  lattice steps scores -80.34 dB behind the network model, as good as the
  truth (-80.39): a lattice step and the choice of the wing compensate each
  other, and the network model's -80 dB floor cannot separate them. The exact
  relation can: -89.9 dB against -128.0 dB.
- 132.3 kHz resisted: output samples on exact entries stayed at -72.6 dB
  (exact), -72.2 dB (after wing lowered) or -67.7 dB (before wing lowered)
  while the other two thirds scored -80.3 dB. A free fit of 37 taps for those
  samples reached -80.29 dB and showed the after wing lowered by exactly one
  entry on every tap *but the last*. That is rule 3.
- 66.15 kHz: -75.05 dB at best with wing rules, -80.34 dB once the table
  position of the input converter was accumulated (rule 2).
- A first version of the clock simulation that did not carry the creep into
  the next block gave the wrong output sign at 96 and 64 kHz.
- Not everything is determined by the network model alone: before the exact
  relation existed, the split of the delays at 96 kHz was only known modulo
  5 lattice steps (7029, 7034 and 7030 scored -79.8 to -80.3 dB with fitting
  rules). Only the exact relation separates such twins (the one at 192 kHz
  above by 38 dB).

## 6. The rule at a rate nobody measured

**44.1 kHz and above (for example 705.6 or 768 kHz). INFERRED from 19 rates
without exception**, among them rates no product meets (45, 47.25, 55.125,
110.25 kHz): apply section 3 as it stands. `converters.describe(768000)`:
latency 768, L/M = 2560/147, `a` = 45068, `b` = 297472, fixed part 4.0319 ms,
clocks high and high. For 705.6 kHz: latency 704, `a` = 283, `b` = 1152, fixed
part 3.0315 ms, both clocks exact. The only part that is not a closed formula
is the pair of clock signs; `clock_signs` computes it, and a product that does
not need to null against the reference may ignore it (it is worth 5 to 9 dB at
a floor of -80 dB and is inaudible).

**Below 44.1 kHz (for example 32 kHz). INFERRED, unverified, and it cannot be
verified with the campaign's tools.** The two converters exchange roles:

- the input converter becomes an up-converter: kernel on the *host* grid (0.9
  of the host Nyquist frequency, 17 host samples per wing), gain 1,
  `X_in = 28` host samples;
- the output converter becomes a down-converter: table stepped by
  `4096 * rate / 44100` per internal sample, gain `rate / 44100`, look-ahead
  `floor(18 * 44100 / rate + 10)` internal samples in place of 28;
- in other words the cut-off and the 17 crossings follow **the lower of the
  two rates**, as they do in the classic design; the delay formulas, the
  latency rule and the three rules for exact entries stay as they are.
- `converters.describe(32000)`: latency 32, L/M = 320/441, `a` = 12469,
  `b` = 39104, fixed part 148.87 samples = 4.652 ms, clocks low and low.
- OPEN there: whether a whole ratio downwards (22.05 kHz) also gets one block
  of output delay (`converters.py` assumes so), and whether the plug-in
  converts at all below 44.1 kHz rather than running its network at the host
  rate.

A product that only has to sound like the reference needs, at any rate: the
44.1 kHz core, a band limit at 0.9 of the lower Nyquist frequency on the way in
and out, the gain `min(1, 44100 / rate)` in and `min(1, rate / 44100)` out, and
the delays of section 3.3. The table, its truncation and the clocks matter only
for a null.

## 7. What is not established

- Macro above 0 at other rates (not repeatable; section 0).
- Rates below 44.1 kHz (section 6).
- The first milliseconds after the start of processing, before a clock has
  drifted off its exact value: the shortest warm-up tested is 4.3 s. In the
  simulation the sign is final within two blocks.
- Single or double precision of the table entries, and the single-precision
  details of the converter sums (the -131 dB floor).
- Why the input side carries `X_in + 1` host samples and loses one internal
  sample (the simplest reading: the converter is primed with one sample more
  than its look-ahead and its first output is not used), and the mechanism
  behind the second block of output delay. Only the resulting times are
